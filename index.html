How I lead product

Approach

Administrative burden is a design choice, which means it can be designed out. These are the habits I bring to every team, with an example from the work.

  1. Start with the people the system fails

    Before Coverage Checker, we surveyed care teams. 83% named coverage and prior authorization as their top barrier, against 18% who named clinical knowledge. That finding set the strategy.

  2. Ship small, learn, and iterate

    We started with a PDF guide instead of software and revised it every quarter. When we built the MVP in 2024, it stood on what three years of updates had taught us about what clinicians needed.

  3. Co-design with the people who do the work

    7,000+ hours of participatory design with patients, caregivers, and clinicians, including co-design sessions that shaped the Coverage Checker MVP.

  4. Align partners around shared outcomes

    Good tools depend on many hands: clinicians, pharmacists, designers, engineers, payers, and manufacturers. I set direction, make the tradeoffs explicit, and keep everyone pointed at what changes for patients.

  5. Measure what changes for people

    Searches, downloads, and whether clinicians would recommend the tool to a colleague tell us if it works. Publishing results in peer-reviewed journals keeps us honest.

  6. Grow the team and the practice

    I built a UX team from 1 to 6 and manage people as well as products. Social work training shapes how I lead: listen first, name harm plainly, and make room for disagreement.

Background

About

Prior authorizations, coverage rules, and forms that don't talk to each other aren't neutral. They're administrative burden, and they cost people time, money, and dignity while wearing down the workers who serve them. The same burden keeps people from health coverage and public benefits. My work is about lifting it.

At Michigan Medicine, I lead informatics design and product for statewide collaborative quality initiatives. I set product direction, run agile delivery with designers, developers, and clinical experts, and use research and usage data to decide what to build next. Coverage Checker began as one workflow for diabetes coverage and prior authorization and grew into a suite supporting more than 20 products (Annals of Family Medicine, 2025). Along the way I grew our digital portfolio from 6 to nearly 90 tools and built a UX team from 1 to 6.

I bring a human-computer interaction and UX research background from the University of Michigan School of Information, and I'm earning my MSW at U-M on the path to clinical licensure. Social work gives me language for what I've seen in health tech: moral injury, structural harm, and the power of collective action. I want to bring that lens, and a careful approach to AI, to civic technology: public services that are easier to navigate and kinder to the people inside them.
